What Fair Vitals is

Fair Vitals estimates your heart rate from a short video of your face. On video it had never seen before, it cut the error of the standard method roughly in half. It also tells you when it isn't sure, instead of guessing.

Camera-based heart rate tends to work worse on darker skin, so we tried to keep that in mind from the start. We haven't proved the app is fair yet, and the limits section below says why.

A note on what's actually new here: we built and tested all of this ourselves, but the main ideas aren't new to the field. Others have published work on improving POS with a learned model, on scoring signal quality, and on using the background to correct for lighting. What's ours is how we put them together, what we found, and how carefully we tested it.

How it works

We cut your video into 10-second pieces that overlap, starting a new one every 5 seconds. Each piece gets its own heart rate and its own trust score. We average the trust scores, and only show a heart rate if that average is high enough. The number comes from the pieces that passed.

POS is a well-known way to pull a pulse out of skin color without any training. Our refiner starts out doing exactly what POS does and only learns a correction on top.

Data

We trained on two public datasets, UBFC-rPPG (42 people) and UBFC-Phys (23 people). We always tested on people the model hadn't trained on. A third dataset, TU Eindhoven (3 people, with changing light and movement), we kept aside completely and never trained anything on.

What we found

The refiner does better on video it hasn't seen

On TU Eindhoven, the refiner's error was about half that of POS. On the two datasets it trained on, it does about as well as POS, which makes sense, since it starts out as POS.

Average error in BPM. On TU Eindhoven the share of readings within 5 BPM went from 60% to 75%, and all three people got better results (7.2 to 3.6, 12.0 to 5.2, and 17.6 to 8.6). It helped most with changing light and movement; in one movement test the error dropped from 37.7 to 9.4. On the two UBFC datasets the difference from POS isn't statistically meaningful.

The trust model never looks at skin tone

To decide whether to trust a reading, the trust model looks at how much the different methods disagree, how clean the signal is, and how much movement, lighting change and flicker there was. We left skin tone and brightness out on purpose, so it can't decide based on them.

A flickering lamp can look like a heartbeat

At one point both POS and the CNN were reading about 125 BPM where there was no such pulse. It turned out to be the light flickering. The giveaway was the wall behind the person: it "pulsed" at the same rate, and a real heartbeat doesn't show up on a wall. The app now checks for this and corrects it.

Why the hardest case was hard

The person the app struggled with most had the darkest skin in the test set, and their face was underexposed in front of a bright background (face brightness around 48 out of 255, background around 116). Their pulse changed the face's brightness by only about 0.2%, which is about a tenth of the smallest brightness step a camera pixel can record, and the 125 BPM flicker was on top of that. It's a measured example of why this is harder on darker skin, and why good lighting matters so much.

How we kept ourselves honest

  • Nobody's video was ever used for both training and testing.
  • Our confidence intervals count people, not individual readings.
  • One dataset stayed completely untouched until the final test.
  • We decided how to judge each result before seeing it.
  • We took back a claim when more data disagreed. With 10 people the refiner looked clearly better on UBFC-Phys (−1.60 BPM); with 23 people it was a tie.

What didn't work

  • Neural networks trained on computer-generated faces didn't beat POS on real faces, even after we fine-tuned them on real people (they overfit).
  • A bigger network didn't help either. The problem is the gap between generated and real video, not the size of the model.
  • Letting a model choose between POS and the CNN for each piece of video didn't beat plain POS. A test we ran beforehand had predicted that.
  • Smoothing readings based on quality helped a little on hard videos but hurt a little on easy ones. The refiner replaced it.

What we haven't proved

Fair Vitals is a student research project, not a medical device. Please don't use it to make decisions about your health.

  1. That it's fair across skin tones. In our data, skin tone is tangled up with how hard each dataset is, measured skin tone changes with lighting, and the groups are small. We designed for fairness and checked for it, but that's not the same as proving it.
  2. That it works on phone videos. All of our numbers come from webcams and lab cameras. We haven't tested phone auto-exposure, compression or changing frame rates, even though most people will use a phone here.
  3. That the refiner beats POS in general. It ties on UBFC. The clear win is on TU Eindhoven, which is only 3 people from one lab.
  4. That the trust model holds up in new situations. For the hardest test person it still let some wrong readings through (it showed 14% of them, and those were about 10 BPM off in an earlier run).
  5. How accurate the whole app is on UBFC. Our full-app check used models trained on everyone in UBFC, so those numbers aren't a fair test. Only the TU Eindhoven result is.

Limits of the website

  • Videos need to be at least 10 seconds long. We only look at the first 60 seconds, and files over 200 MB won't upload.
  • Your face needs to be in view, still and evenly lit. Moving around, dim or changing light, a bright window behind you, or a flickering lamp or screen will make it ask you to try again more often.
  • The trust score is the average over all the 10-second pieces, and you only get a heart rate when it's at least 85%. That threshold was picked for single pieces, so on an average it's a bit stricter than in our tests. The heart rate is the middle value of the pieces that passed, so one odd piece can't drag it off.
  • It runs on an ordinary server without a graphics card, so a video can take a minute or more, and it handles one video at a time.
  • We look at how bright your face is only to suggest better light. It never changes the reading or whether we trust it.
  • Your video is deleted as soon as it's been analyzed. If you're logged in and press Save, we keep the heart rate and the time, nothing else.

What we'd do next

To really test fairness, we'd need people across skin tones measured the same way. Two ways to get there:

A small test at home

Same phone, same lighting and same steps for everyone, with a fingertip pulse oximeter as the reference. Then compare how far off the app is for each skin-tone group, and against POS.

Bigger public datasets

MMPD (33 people filmed on phones, Fitzpatrick skin types 3–6) and VitalVideos (hundreds of people with skin type labels). Both need a signed academic agreement.
