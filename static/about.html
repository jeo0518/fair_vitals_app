About

Heart rate from a selfie, with an honest answer when it can't tell.

Fair Vitals estimates heart rate from a short video of your face. On video it had never seen, it halved the error of the standard method. It knows when not to trust itself, and it was designed and evaluated with skin-tone fairness in mind, including an honest account of where it still falls short.

2×lower error than standard POS on an unseen dataset (11.1 → 5.2 BPM)
90%of the readings it accepts are within 5 BPM on that dataset
0skin-tone or brightness inputs in the decision to trust a reading

On "novelty". Most of this work is original to our project: we built, tested and measured it ourselves. It is not necessarily new to the field. Learned refinement of POS, signal-quality scoring and background-based light correction have all been explored in published camera-pulse (rPPG) research. What is ours is the combination, the findings and the rigour of the evaluation.

Architecture

How it works

Your video is cut into overlapping 10-second windows, a new one every 5 seconds. Each window gets a heart rate and a trust score, and only trusted readings are combined into the number you see.

POS ("plane orthogonal to skin") is a standard, training-free way to pull the pulse out of skin colour. The refiner starts out as exactly POS before training and learns only a correction on top of it.

Data

Trained on UBFC-rPPG (42 people) and UBFC-Phys (23 people), evaluated with person-level cross-validation, so no person is ever in both training and testing. TU Eindhoven (3 people, with lighting changes and motion) was held out completely and never used to train anything.

What's new in this project

What we found

1. A learned POS refiner that generalizes

On TU Eindhoven, a dataset it never saw, the refiner halved the error. On the datasets it was trained on it ties POS, which is expected: it starts as POS and only learns a correction.

DatasetPOSPOS + flicker correctionRefiner
UBFC-rPPG (cross-validated, 42 people)1.211.231.35

Mean absolute error in BPM. On TU, readings within 5 BPM went from 60% (POS) to 75% (refiner), and all three participants improved (P1 7.2 → 3.6, P2 12.0 → 5.2, P3 17.6 → 8.6). The biggest gains were under changing light and motion (one motion scenario: 37.7 → 9.4 BPM). On the UBFC datasets the difference from POS is a statistical tie (95% bootstrap intervals over people include zero).

2. A skin-tone-blind trust model

The trust model predicts whether a reading is reliable from how much the different methods disagree, signal quality, motion, lighting and flicker. Skin tone and brightness are deliberately left out as inputs, so the decision to trust a reading can't be based on them directly.

Trust and error correlate at −0.57: the less the app trusts a reading, the more wrong it tends to be.

3. The flicker discovery

Flickering light created a fake "pulse" of about 125 BPM that fooled both POS and the CNN. We confirmed it by comparing the face with the background around it: the background pulsed at the same rate, which a real heartbeat never does. The app now detects this and corrects for it.

4. A physical explanation for the hardest case

The hardest test person (TU participant P3, the darkest skin in the test set) was filmed with an under-exposed face against a bright background: face brightness about 48 out of 255, background about 116. The pulse was only about 0.2% of face brightness, roughly a tenth of one step in pixel brightness, with the 125 BPM flicker on top. It is a concrete, measured illustration of why camera pulse measurement is harder on darker skin, and why exposure matters.

5. A careful evaluation

  • Person-level cross-validation: no person in both training and test.
  • Bootstrap confidence intervals over people, not over individual readings.
  • A fully held-out dataset (TU Eindhoven).
  • Decision rules set before seeing the results.
  • Retiring our own claims when more data disagreed: an early "significantly better on UBFC-Phys" (−1.60 BPM with 10 people) became a tie with 23 people.

6. What didn't work

  • CNNs trained on synthetic faces did not beat POS on real faces, even after fine-tuning on real people, which overfit.
  • A larger network made no difference (a statistical tie), so the bottleneck is the gap between synthetic and real video, not model size.
  • Learning to pick POS or the CNN for each window did not beat POS, matching a ceiling test run in advance.
  • Smoothing readings by quality helped hard video slightly but cost a little on clean video, and the refiner replaced it.

Limitations

What has not been proved

Fair Vitals is a research prototype, not a medical device. Don't use it to make health decisions.

  1. Fairness across skin tones. Skin tone is mixed up with how hard each dataset is, measured skin tone shifts with lighting, and the groups are small. We built for fairness and checked for it; we have not proved it.
  2. That it works on phone videos. Every number above comes from webcams or lab cameras. Phone auto-exposure, compression and changing frame rates are untested, and this website is mostly used with phone videos.
  3. That the refiner beats POS in general. It ties on the UBFC datasets; the clear win is on TU Eindhoven, which is only 3 people from one lab.
  4. That the trust model holds up in new conditions. It still accepted some wrong readings for the hardest test person (14% kept, about 10 BPM off in an earlier run).
  5. End-to-end accuracy on UBFC. The full-app check used models trained on all UBFC people, so its UBFC numbers are in-sample. Only its TU Eindhoven result is a clean test.

Limits of this website

  • It needs at least 10 seconds of video and analyses at most the first 60 seconds. Videos larger than 200 MB are rejected.
  • Your face needs to be visible, still and evenly lit. Movement, dim or changing light, a bright window behind you, or flickering lamps and screens make readings less reliable, and you'll more often be asked to retake.
  • The heart rate shown is the middle value of the trusted 10-second readings. The trust score is the middle value across all readings, so a result can be shown with a fairly low overall score when only some readings passed.
  • The analysis runs on a CPU server, so it can take a minute or more, and the server handles one video at a time.
  • A separate rule looks at face brightness only to suggest better light ("your face looks too dark"); it never changes the reading or the trust decision.
  • Your video is uploaded to the server for analysis and deleted right after; the app keeps nothing.

Next steps

How fairness could be tested properly

Controlled home pilot

The same phone, lighting and steps for everyone, people across skin tones, and a fingertip pulse oximeter as the reference. Then compare each group's error, and the gap between groups, for the app versus POS.

Balanced public datasets

MMPD (33 people, phone video, Fitzpatrick skin types 3–6) and VitalVideos (hundreds of people with Fitzpatrick labels). Both need a signed academic access agreement.
